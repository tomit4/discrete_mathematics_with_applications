15. Show that the function $f: \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$ defined by the formula
    $f(x) = 2x - 3$ is increasing on the set of real numbers.

**Proof:**

Suppose $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, where $x_1 < x_2$.

Let $f(x) = 2x - 3$.

It must be shown that $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$.

Since $x_1 < x_2$, it follows that:

$$ 2x_1 < 2x_2 $$

Since multiplying by a positive number on both sides of an inequality does not
change the direction of the inequality.

It also follows that:

$$ 2x_1 - 3 < 2x_2 - 3 $$

Since subtracting an number on both sides of an inequality does not change the
direction of the inequality.

Thus, by substitution:

$$ f(x_1) < f(x_2) $$

This is what was to be shown.

Since addition of a number on both sides of an inequality does not change the

Thus, by substitution:

17. Let $h$ be the function from $\mathbf{R}$ to $\mathbf{R}$ defined by the
    formula $h(x) = x^2$ for each real number $x$

a. Show that $h$ is decreasing on the set of real numbers less than zero.

**Proof:**

Suppose $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, where $x_1 < x_2 < 0$.

Let $h(x) = x^2$.

It must be shown that $h(x_1) > h(x_2)$.

Since $x_1 < x_2$, it follows that when both sides of the inequality are
multiplied by $x_1$:

$$ (x_1)^2 > x_1x_2 $$

Since $x_1 < 0$, the direction of the inequality changes when multiplying both
sides by $x_1$.

Similarly, when multiplying both sides of the inequality by $x_2$:

$$ x_1x_2 > (x_2)^2 $$

Again, this flips the inequality because $x_2 < 0$.

Now, this means that:

$$ (x_1)^2 > x_1x_2 > (x_2)^2 $$

And by the laws of transitivity:

$$ (x_1)^2 > (x_2)^2 $$

Then, by substitution:

$$ h(x_1) > h(x_2) $$

This is what was to be shown.

Let $k(x) = \dfrac{(x - 1)}{x}$

It must be shown that $k(x_1) < k(x_2)$.

Since $x_1 < x_2$, it follows that if you multiply both sides by $-1$:

$$ -x_2 < -x_1 $$

Then, by adding $x_2x_1$ to both sides:

$$ x_2x_1 - x_2 < x_2x_1 - x_1 $$

Then, by factoring:

$$ x_2(x_1 - 1) < x_1(x_2 - 1) $$

Then divide both sides by $x_1$:

$$ \frac{x_2(x_1 - 1)}{x_1} < (x_2 - 1) $$

Note that this does not change the direction of the inequality since $x_1 > 0$.

Then divide both sides by $x_2$:

$$ \frac{(x_1 - 1)}{x_1} < \frac{(x_2 - 1)}{x_2} $$

Again, this does not change the direction of the inequality since $x_2 > 0$.

Finally, by substitution:

$$ k(x_1) < k(x_2) $$

This is what was to be shown.

19. Show that if a function $f: \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$ is increasing, then
    $f$ is one-to-one.

**Proof:**

Suppose there is a function $f: \mathbf{R} \to\mathbf{R}$ that is increasing.

By definition for increasing, this means that given any
$x_1, x_2 \in \mathbf{R}$ where $x_1 < x_2$, that $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$. Similarly,
if $x_1 > x_2$, that $f(x_1) > f(x_2)$.

It must be shown that $f$ is one-to-one, or equivalently, it must be shown that
if $x_1 \neq x_2$, then $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.

Suppose $x_1 \neq x_2$, by the trichotomy law, this means that either
$x_1 > x_2$ or $x_1 < x_2$.

_Case ($x_1 < x_2$):_

Since $x_1 < x_2$, it follows that $x_1 \neq x_2$, therefore it suffices to show
that $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.

Similarly, by the supposition, it is known that if $x_1 < x_2$, then
$f(x_1) < f(x_2)$, and therefore $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.

_Case ($x_1 > x_2$):_

Since $x_1 > x_2$, it follows that $x_1 \neq x_2$, therefore it suffices to show
that $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.

Similarly, by the supposition, it is known that if $x_1 > x_2$, then
$f(x_1) > f(x_2)$, and therefore $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.

_Conclusion:_

In both cases, it has been demonstrated that $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$ when
$x_1 \neq x_2$.

This is what was to be shown.

20. Given real-valued functions $f$ and $g$ with the same domain $D$, the sum of
    $f$ and $g$, denoted $f + g$, is defined as follows:

For each real number $x$, $(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)$.

Show that if $f$ and $g$ are both increasing on a set $S$, then $f + g$ is also
increasing on $S$.

**Proof:**

Suppose $f$ and $g$ are both functions on the same domain $D$, where both $f$
and $g$ are increasing on a set $S$.

Let $x_1, x_2 \in S$ where $x_1 < x_2$. Since $f$ and $g$ are increasing, this
means that $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$ and $g(x_1) < g(x_2)$.

It must be shown that $(f + g)(x_1) < (f + g)(x_2)$.

Since $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$, and $g(x_1) < g(x_2)$, it follows that:

$$ f(x_1) + g(x_1) < f(x_2) + g(x_2) $$

This does not change the inequality specifically because $g(x_1) < g(x_2)$.

Now, by definition for $(f + g)$, it follows by substitution that:

$$ (f + g)(x_1) < (f + g)(x_2) $$

Which is what was to be shown.

a. Let $m$ be a positive integer, and define $f(x) = x^m$ for each nonnegative

For reference the binomial theorem states:

**Theorem 9.7.2 Binomial Theorem**

Given any real numbers $a$ and $b$ and any nonnegative integer $n$,

$$ (a + b)^n = \sum_{k = 0}^{n}{\binom{n}{k}a^{n - k}b^k} $$

$$ = a^n + \binom{n}{1}a^{n - 1}b^1 + \binom{n}{2}a^{n - 2}b^2 + \cdots + \binom{n}{n - 1}a^1b^{n - 1} + b^n $$

**Proof:**

Suppose $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $m > 0$.

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$ where $x \geq 0$, and let $f$ be a function such that:

$$ f(x) = x^m $$

Now, let $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ where $0 \leq x_1 < x_2$.

It must be shown that $f$ is increasing, or equivalently $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$.

Since $x_1 < x_2$, it follows that:

$$ x_2 = x_1 + h $$

For some $h \in \mathbb{R}$, and $h > 0$.

Thus $(x_2)^m$ becomes:

$$ (x_2)^m = (x_1 + h)^m $$

By the binomial theorem, this is equal to:

$$ (x_1 + h)^m = \sum_{k = 0}^{m}{\binom{m}{k}(x_1)^{m - k}h^k} $$

Now, this summation is positive since $x_1 \geq 0$, and $h > 0$, and the
expansion of this summation yields:

$$ = (x_1)^m + \binom{m}{1}(x_1)^{m - 1}h + \binom{m}{2}(x_1)^{m - 2}h^2 + \cdots + \binom{m}{m - 1}(x_1)h^{m - 1} + h^m $$

Thus it follows that:

$$ (x_2)^m = (x_1 + h)^m = (x_1)^m + \text{ some positive number} $$

And thus:

$$ (x_2)^m = (x_1)^m + \text{ some positive number} > (x_1)^m $$

More succinctly, this means:

$$ (x_2)^m > (x_1)^m $$

And by the definition for $f$:

$$ f(x_2) > f(x_1) $$

$$ f(x_1) < f(x_2) $$

This is what was to be shown.

**Proof:**

Suppose $m, n \in \mathbf{Z}$ where $m > 0$, and $n > 0$.

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$, such that $x \geq 0$, and let $g$ be a function such
that:

$$ g(x) = x^{\frac{m}{n}} $$

It must be shown that $g$ is an increasing function.

Let $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, where $0 \leq x_1 < x_2$.

It must be shown that $g(x_1) < g(x_2)$, or equivalently
$(x_1)^{\frac{m}{n}} < (x_2)^{\frac{m}{n}}$.

Note that $x^{\frac{m}{n}}$ can be expressed as
$\left(x^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)^m$. This is useful because in part (a), it was
shown that if $x_1 < x_2$, then $(x_1)^m < (x_2)^m$.

Thus it suffices to show that $(x_1)^{\frac{1}{n}} < (x_2)^{\frac{1}{n}}$.

Suppose $(x_1)^{\frac{1}{n}} \geq (x_2)^{\frac{1}{n}}$.

A contradiction to this supposition must be derived.

Raising both sides of this inequality by $n$ yields:

$$ \left((x_1)^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)^n \geq \left((x_2)^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)^n  $$

Since $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $n > 0$, it follows by part (a) that:

$$ x_1 \geq x_2 $$

But this contradicts the fact that $x_1 < x_2$.

Thus the supposition is false, and therefore
$(x_1)^{\frac{1}{n}} < (x_2)^{\frac{1}{n}}$, and it can be concluded that $g$ is

24. Let $f$ be a real-valued function of a real variable. Show that if $f$ is
    decreasing on a set $S$ and if $M$ is any positive real number, then $Mf$ is
    decreasing on $S$.

**Proof:**

Suppose $f$ is a function defined as $f: \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$, where $f$
is decreasing on a set $S$.

Let $M \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $M > 0$.

Let $x_1, x_2 \in S$, where $x_1 < x_2$.

It must shown that $Mf$ is decreasing on $S$, or equivalently that
$M \cdot f(x_1) > M \cdot f(x_2)$.

Since $f$ is decreasing, this means that $f(x_1) > f(x_2)$.

It follows that, since $M > 0$:

$$ M \cdot f(x_1) > M \cdot f(x_2) $$

Note that the direction of the inequality does not change specifically because
$M > 0$.

This is what was to be shown, and it can be concluded that $Mf$ is decreasing on
$S$.
