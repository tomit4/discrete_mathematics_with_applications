15. Show that the function $f: \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$ defined by the formula
    $f(x) = 2x - 3$ is increasing on the set of real numbers.

**Proof:**

Suppose $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, where $x_1 < x_2$.

Let $f(x) = 2x - 3$.

It must be shown that $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$.

Since $x_1 < x_2$, it follows that:

$$ 2x_1 < 2x_2 $$

Since multiplying by a positive number on both sides of an inequality does not
change the direction of the inequality.

It also follows that:

$$ 2x_1 - 3 < 2x_2 - 3 $$

Since subtracting an number on both sides of an inequality does not change the
direction of the inequality.

Thus, by substitution:

$$ f(x_1) < f(x_2) $$

This is what was to be shown.

19. Show that if a function $f: \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$ is increasing, then
    $f$ is one-to-one.

**Proof:**

Suppose there is a function $f: \mathbf{R} \to\mathbf{R}$ that is increasing.

By definition for increasing, this means that given any
$x_1, x_2 \in \mathbf{R}$ where $x_1 < x_2$, that $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$. Similarly,
if $x_1 > x_2$, that $f(x_1) > f(x_2)$.

It must be shown that $f$ is one-to-one, or equivalently, it must be shown that
if $x_1 \neq x_2$, then $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.

Suppose $x_1 \neq x_2$, by the trichotomy law, this means that either
$x_1 > x_2$ or $x_1 < x_2$.

_Case ($x_1 < x_2$):_

Since $x_1 < x_2$, it follows that $x_1 \neq x_2$, therefore it suffices to show
that $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.

Similarly, by the supposition, it is known that if $x_1 < x_2$, then
$f(x_1) < f(x_2)$, and therefore $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.

_Case ($x_1 > x_2$):_

Since $x_1 > x_2$, it follows that $x_1 \neq x_2$, therefore it suffices to show
that $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.

Similarly, by the supposition, it is known that if $x_1 > x_2$, then
$f(x_1) > f(x_2)$, and therefore $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.

_Conclusion:_

In both cases, it has been demonstrated that $f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$ when
$x_1 \neq x_2$.

This is what was to be shown.

For reference the binomial theorem states:

**Theorem 9.7.2 Binomial Theorem**

Given any real numbers $a$ and $b$ and any nonnegative integer $n$,

$$ (a + b)^n = \sum_{k = 0}^{n}{\binom{n}{k}a^{n - k}b^k} $$

$$ = a^n + \binom{n}{1}a^{n - 1}b^1 + \binom{n}{2}a^{n - 2}b^2 + \cdots + \binom{n}{n - 1}a^1b^{n - 1} + b^n $$

**Proof:**

Suppose $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $m > 0$.

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$ where $x \geq 0$, and let $f$ be a function such that:

$$ f(x) = x^m $$

Now, let $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ where $0 \leq x_1 < x_2$.

It must be shown that $f$ is increasing, or equivalently $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$.

Since $x_1 < x_2$, it follows that:

$$ x_2 = x_1 + h $$

For some $h \in \mathbb{R}$, and $h > 0$.

Thus $(x_2)^m$ becomes:

$$ (x_2)^m = (x_1 + h)^m $$

By the binomial theorem, this is equal to:

$$ (x_1 + h)^m = \sum_{k = 0}^{m}{\binom{m}{k}(x_1)^{m - k}h^k} $$

Now, this summation is positive since $x_1 \geq 0$, and $h > 0$, and the
expansion of this summation yields:

$$ = (x_1)^m + \binom{m}{1}(x_1)^{m - 1}h + \binom{m}{2}(x_1)^{m - 2}h^2 + \cdots + \binom{m}{m - 1}(x_1)h^{m - 1} + h^m $$

Thus it follows that:

$$ (x_2)^m = (x_1 + h)^m = (x_1)^m + \text{ some positive number} $$

And thus:

$$ (x_2)^m = (x_1)^m + \text{ some positive number} > (x_1)^m $$

More succinctly, this means:

$$ (x_2)^m > (x_1)^m $$

And by the definition for $f$:

$$ f(x_2) > f(x_1) $$

$$ f(x_1) < f(x_2) $$

This is what was to be shown.

1. The following is a formal definition for $\Omega$-notation, written using
   quantifiers and variables: $f(n)$ is $\Omega(g(n))$ if, and only if,
   $\exists$ positive real numbers $a$ and $A$ such that $\forall n \geq a$,

$$ Ag(n) \leq f(n) $$

a. Write the formal negation for the definition using the symbols $\forall$ and
$\exists$.

First, the formal notation is:

$$ f(n) \text{ is } \Omega(g(n)) \Leftrightarrow \exists a, A \in \mathbf{R}^+ | \forall n \geq a, Ag(n) \leq f(n) $$

Then, the formal negation is:

$$ f(n) \text{ is not } \Omega(g(n)) \Leftrightarrow \forall a, A \in \mathbf{R}^+, \exists n \geq a | Ag(n) > f(n) $$

b. Restate the negation less formally without using the symbols $\forall$ and
$\exists$ or the words "for any," "for every," or "there exists."

$f(n)$ is not $\Omega(g(n))$ if, and only if, no matter what positive real
numbers $a$ and $A$ are chosen, it is possible to find an integer $n$ that is
greater than or equal to $a$ where $Ag(n) > f(n)$.

2. The following is a formal definition for $O$-notation, written using
   quantifiers and variables: $f(n)$ is $O(g(n))$ if, and only if, $\exists$
   positive real numbers $b$ and $B$ such that $\forall n \geq b$,

$$ 0 \leq f(n) \leq Bg(n) $$

a. Write the formal negation for the definition using the symbols $\forall$ and
$\exists$.

First, the formal notation is:

$$ f(n) \text{ is } O(g(n)) \Leftrightarrow \exists b, B \in \mathbf{R}^+ | \forall n \geq b, 0 \leq f(n) \leq Bg(n) $$

Then, the negation is:

$$ f(n) \text{ is not } O(g(n)) \Leftrightarrow \forall b, B \in \mathbf{R}^+, \exists n \geq b | (0 > f(n) \vee f(n) > Bg(n)) $$

b. Restate the negation less formally without using the symbols $\forall$ and
$\exists$ or the words "for any," "for every," or "there exists."

$f(n)$ is not $O(g(n))$ if, and only if, no matter what positive real numbers
$b$ and $B$, it is possible to find an integer $n$ that is greater than or equal
to $b$ where $0 > f(n)$ or $f(n) > Bg(n)$.

   quantifiers and variables: $f(n)$ is $\Theta(g(n))$ if, and only if,

   $\forall n \geq k$,

$$ Ag(n) \leq f(n) \leq Bg(n) $$

$$ g(n) \leq \frac{f(n)}{A} $$

$$ \frac{f(n)}{B} \leq g(n) $$

    the same set of nonnegative integers and if $f(n) \geq 0$ and $g(n) \geq 0$

nonnegative integers.

Thus two proofs must be provided:

_Proof (1<sup>st<sup> side of $\Leftrightarrow$):_

The following expression is what is to be shown:

$$ f(n) \text{ is } \Theta(g(n)) \to f(n) \text{ is } \Omega(g(n)) \wedge f(n) \text{ is } O(g(n)) $$

$$ Ag(n) \leq f(n) \leq Bg(n) \quad \text{ for every integer } n \geq k $$

$$ A'g(n) \leq f(n) $$

Since $A$ is a positive real number, let $A' = A$, and thus, by the supposition

To show that $f(n)$ is $O(g(n))$, it must be shown that there exists some
positive real number $B'$ such that:

$$ 0 \leq f(n) \leq B'g(n) $$

Now, since $A$ is positive, and $g(n) \geq 0$ it follows that $0 \leq Ag(n)$.
Furthermore, since $B$ is a positive real number, let $B' = B$. It follows by
the supposition (again, since $f(n)$ is $\Theta(g(n))$), that $f(n)$ is also
$O(g(n))$.

Therefore, this 1<sup>st</sup> side of the $\Leftrightarrow$ of the main proof
has been demonstrated.

_Proof (2<sup>nd<sup> side of $\Leftrightarrow$):_

The following expression is what is to be shown:

$$ f(n) \text{ is } \Omega(g(n)) \wedge f(n) \text{ is } O(g(n)) \to f(n) \text{ is } \Theta(g(n)) $$

Suppose $f(n)$ is $\Omega(g(n))$ and $f(n)$ is $O(g(n))$.

By definition for $\Omega$-notation, this means there exists some positive real
numbers $A$ and $a \geq r$ such that:

$$ Ag(n) \leq f(n) \quad \text{ for every integer } n \geq a $$

Furthermore, by definition for $O$-notation, this means there exists positive
real numbers $B$ and $b \geq r$ such that:

$$ 0 \leq f(n) \leq Bg(n) \quad \text{ for every integer } n \geq b $$

To show that $f(n)$ is $\Theta(g(n))$, it must be shown there exists positive

$$ A'g(n) \leq f(n) \leq B'g(n) \quad \text{ for every integer } n \geq k $$

Now, since it has been shown that $Ag(n) \leq f(n)$ (since $f(n)$ is
$\Omega(g(n))$), simply let $A = A'$. Additionally, since $A'$ is (now) a

Thus, it has been shown that $f(n)$ is $\Theta(g(n))$, and this 2<sup>nd</sup>
side of the main proof has been demonstrated.

Since both sides of the $\Leftrightarrow$ statement have been demonstrated, the
main proof has been shown.

So:

b. Use mathematical induction to verify the correctness of the formula.

**Proof (by mathematical induction):**

Suppose $n \in \mathbf{Z}$, with $n \geq 1$.

Let $P(n)$ be the equation:

$$ E_n = \frac{1}{4}n^2 + \frac{3}{4}n - 1 $$

It is to be shown that $P(n)$ holds for all $n \geq 1$.

_Basis Step:_

Prove $P(1)$, that is:

$$ E_1 = \frac{1}{4}(1)^2 + \frac{3}{4}(1) - 1 $$

$$ = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{4} - 1 $$

$$ = \frac{4}{4} - 1 $$

$$ = 1 - 1 $$

$$ = 0 $$

By Example 11.3.7, this is known to be true, thus $P(1)$ is true.

_Inductive Step:_

Suppose $k \in \mathbf{Z}$, with $k \geq 1$.

Suppose $P(k)$, that is:

$$ E_k = \frac{1}{4}k^2 + \frac{3}{4}k - 1 $$

This is the inductive hypothesis.

Prove $P(k + 1)$, that is:

$$ E_{k + 1} = \frac{1}{4}(k + 1)^2 + \frac{3}{4}(k + 1) - 1 $$

Equivalently, prove that $E_{k + 1}$:

$$ = \frac{1}{4}(k + 1)(k + 1) + \frac{3}{4}(k + 1) - 1 $$

$$ = \frac{1}{4}(k^2 + 2k + 1) + \frac{3}{4}(k + 1) - 1 $$

$$ = \frac{1}{4}k^2 + \frac{1}{2}k + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{4}k + \frac{3}{4} - 1 $$

$$ = \frac{1}{4}k^2 + \frac{5}{4}k + \frac{4}{4} - 1 $$

$$ = \frac{1}{4}k^2 + \frac{5}{4}k + 1 - 1 $$

$$ = \frac{1}{4}k^2 + \frac{5}{4}k $$

Now, by the given recurrence relation, it is known that:

$$ E_k = E_{k - 1} + \frac{k + 1}{2} $$

Equivalently, this means that:

$$ E_{k + 1} = E_k + \frac{k + 2}{2} $$

By the inductive hypothesis this is:

$$ = \frac{1}{4}k^2 + \frac{3}{4}k - 1 + \frac{k + 2}{2} $$

$$ = \frac{1}{4}k^2 + \frac{3}{4}k - 1 + \frac{1}{2}(k + 2) $$

$$ = \frac{1}{4}k^2 + \frac{3}{4}k - 1 + \frac{1}{2}k + 1 $$

$$ = \frac{1}{4}k^2 + \frac{3}{4}k + \frac{1}{2}k $$

$$ = \frac{1}{4}k^2 + \frac{5}{4}k $$

And this is what was to be shown, thus $P(k + 1)$ is true.

_Conclusion:_

Since both the basis and inductive steps have been demonstrated, it can be
concluded that $P(n)$ is true for all $n \geq 1$.

---

**Algorithm 11.3.2 Selection Sort**

_[Given an array $a[1], a[2], a[3], \dots, a[n]$, this algorithm selects the
smallest element and places it in the first position, then selects the second
smallest element and places it in the second position, and so forth, until the
entire array is sorted. In general, for each $k = 1$ to $n - 1$, the $k$th step
of the algorithm selects the index of the array item with the minimum value from
among $a[k + 1], a[k + 2], a[k + 3], \dots, a[n]$. Once this index is found, the
value of the corresponding array item is interchanged with the value of $a[k]$
unless the index already equals $k$. At the end of execution the array elements
are in order.]_

**Input:** _$n$ [a positive integer], $a[1], a[2], a[3], \dots, a[n]$ [an array
of data items capable of being ordered]_

**Algorithm Body:**

$\textbf{for } k := 1 \textbf{ to } n - 1\\ \ \ \textit{IndexOfMin } := k\\ \ \ \textbf{for } i := k + 1 \textbf{ to } n\\ \ \ \ \ \textbf{if } (a[i] < a[\textit{IndexOfMin}])\\ \ \ \ \ \textbf{then } \textit{IndexOfMin } := i\\ \ \ \textbf{next } i\\ \ \ \textbf{if } \textit{IndexOfMin } \neq k \textbf{ then}\\ \ \ \ \ \textit{Temp } := a[k]\\ \ \ \ \ a[k] := a[\textit{IndexOfMin}]\\ \ \ \ \ a[\textit{IndexOfMin}] := \textit{Temp}\\ \textbf{next } k$

**Output:** _$a[1], a[2], a[3], \dots, a[n]$ [in ascending order]_

The action of selection sort can be represented pictorially as follows:

$a[1] a[2] \cdots \boxed{a[k]} a[k + 1] \cdots a[n]$

Where the boxed $a[k]$ is the $k$th step: Find the index of the array element
with minimum value from among $a[k + 1], \dots, a[n]$. If the value of this
array element is less than the value of $a[k]$, then its value and the value of
$a[k]$ are interchanged.

38. Let $s_n =$ the number of additions and multiplications that are performed
    when Algorithm 11.3.3 is executed for a polynomial of degree $n$. Express
    $s_n$ as a function of $n$.

The number of operations for the inner loop is 1 (multiplication).

When $i = 1$, the inner loop runs $(1) - (1) + 1 = 1$ times. When $i = 2$,
$(2) - (1) + 1 = 2$ times, and so on. When $i = n$, the inner loop runs
$(n) - (1) + 1 = n$ times.

So the inner loop runs this many times:

$$ 1 + 2 + \cdots + n $$

$$ = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2} $$

$$ = \frac{n^2 + n}{2} $$

The outer loop has 1 operation (addition), and the outer loop runs
$n - 1 + 1 = n$ times.

So the total operations for the algorithm is:

$$ s_n = \frac{n^2 + n}{2} + n $$

$$ = \frac{n^2 + n + 2n}{2} $$

$$ = \frac{n^2 + 3n}{2} $$

$$ = \frac{1}{2}n^2 + \frac{3}{2}n $$

By exercise 38 and the theorem on polynomial orders, Algorithm 11.3.3 is

$$ b_{k + 1} = 1 + b_{\lfloor \frac{(k + 1)}{3} \rfloor} $$

By substitution:

$$ = 1 + b_{\lfloor \frac{3m + j}{3} \rfloor} $$

$$ = 1 + b_{\lfloor m + \frac{j}{3} \rfloor} $$

and by the definition of floor, since $0 \leq \dfrac{j}{3} < 1$, and thus
$m \leq m + \dfrac{j}{3} < m + 1$), it follows that:

$$ = 1 + b_m $$

By the inductive hypothesis:

$$ b_m = 1 + \lfloor \log_{3}m \rfloor $$

So:

$$ b_{k + 1} = 1 + b_m = 1 + 1 + \lfloor \log_{3}m \rfloor $$

$$ = 1 + 1 + \lfloor \log_{3}\left(\frac{3m}{3}\right) \rfloor $$

By the definition of logarithm:

$$ = 1 + 1 + \lfloor \log_{3}(3m) - \log_{3}(3) \rfloor $$

$$ = 1 + 1 + \lfloor \log_{3}(3m) - 1 \rfloor $$

$$ = 1 + 1 + \lfloor \log_{3}(3m) \rfloor - 1 $$

$$ = 1 + \lfloor \log_{3}(3m) \rfloor $$

Recall that $k + 1 = 3m + j$ for some $1 \leq m \leq k$ and $0 \leq j < 3$.

By part \(c\), it is known that
$\lfloor \log_{3}(3m) \rfloor = \lfloor \log_{3}(3m + j) \rfloor$, and thus:

$$ = 1 + \lfloor \log_{3}(3m) \rfloor = 1 + \lfloor \log_{3}(3m + j) \rfloor $$

$$ = 1 + \lfloor \log_{3}(k + 1) \rfloor $$

This is what was to be shown, and thus $P(k + 1)$ is true.

Since both the basis and inductive steps have been demonstrated, it can be
concluded that $P(n)$ is true for all $n \geq 2$.
